Self-Supervised Temporal Super-Resolution
Framework With METEOR-Net for Enhancing
Convective Process Reconstruction in
Geostationary Satellite Cloud Data
Xiang Lin
 , Yunying Li
 , Zitong Chen
 , and Xiongwei Kou
Abstract—Next-generation geostationary satellite FY-4A pro-
vides cloud data with a temporal resolution of 15 min, while
shallow convection may evolve into deep convection in less than
30 min. This temporal sparsity limits the satellite’s abil-
ity to capture rapid convective processes, thereby hindering
eﬀective weather monitoring and early warning. Temporal super-
resolution technology is used to address this limitation, but
existing temporal super-resolution methods often directly apply
video frame interpolation (VFI) paradigms designed for RGB
images, resulting in transfer errors. To address such issues, a
novel spatiotemporal self-supervised temporal super-resolution
framework is proposed, which leverages the spatiotemporal
consistency between the regional and full-disk observations of the
FY-4A satellite to establish a temporal super-resolution mapping
relationship without the need for manually labeled data. In
addition, a multichannel encoding and temporal-ﬂow evolution
network for optimized-observation reconstruction (METEOR-
Net) is proposed, which addresses the challenges of multichannel
asynchrony and complex nonlinear evolution in geostationary
satellite cloud data through multichannel collaborative encoding
(MCE), temporal-ﬂow estimation and evolution (TFEE), and
evolution-aware feature fusion mechanisms. To enhance the
focus on convective regions, a new convective region-weighted
loss function and evaluation metrics are designed. Experimental
results demonstrate that this method achieves state-of-the-art
(SOTA) performance in visual quality and convective process
reconstruction accuracy in the FY-4A satellite cloud data tempo-
ral super-resolution reconstruction task, successfully enhancing
the temporal resolution of FY-4A cloud data from 15 to 5 min.
This provides high-resolution satellite cloud data for future
convective weather monitoring and early warning systems.
Index Terms—Multichannel collaborative, self-supervised
learning, temporal super-resolution, temporal-ﬂow evolution.

I. I NTRODUCTION
M
ETEOROLOGICAL satellites are deployed in space
to monitor Earth’s atmosphere, oceans, and surface
conditions. These satellites are equipped with advanced remote
sensing instruments that capture reﬂectance and radiation
intensity at speciﬁed spectral wavelengths and spatiotemporal
resolutions, providing essential information support for various
meteorological operations. Based on their orbital characteris-
tics, meteorological satellites are categorized into two types:
polar-orbiting satellites [1], [2], [3], [4] and geostationary
satellites [5], [6], [7]. Polar-orbiting satellites operate in low-
Earth polar orbits, enabling comprehensive coverage of every
region on Earth within a ﬁxed time period. These satellites
are particularly suitable for long-term climate monitoring and
polar observations. However, their revisit time of approxi-
mately 12 h precludes continuous meteorological monitoring
of speciﬁc regions. In contrast, geostationary satellites oper-
ate in geostationary orbit, allowing sustained observation of
the same region, making them ideal for real-time weather
monitoring and early warning systems.
Next-generation geostationary satellites, including GOES-
16 [5], Himawari-8 [6], and FY-4A [7], are capable of full-disk
observations and providing cloud data. For example, FY-4A
provides geostationary satellite cloud data with a temporal res-
olution of 15 min, based on its 15-min revisit time. While this
temporal resolution is suﬃcient for studying long-term atmo-
spheric trends, climate models, and other gradually evolving
processes [8], [9], [10], [11], it proves inadequate for rapidly
developing convective events, such as convective processes
that may evolve from shallow convection to deep convection
in just 30 min. When relying solely on full-disk data, only
the initial and ﬁnal states of diﬀerent convective stages are
captured, while the actual rapid evolution process within a
revisit period remains unobserved, which limits the tracking
and early warning for convective events. Consequently, con-
vective weather monitoring and warning research is limited
to mesoscale systems where temporal high-resolution satellite
observations are available [12], [13], [14], making large-
scale implementation challenging. This limitation is referred
to as the temporal sparsity dilemma of geostationary satellites.
Solving this dilemma requires temporal high-resolution data
capable of capturing the actual convective evolution process.
