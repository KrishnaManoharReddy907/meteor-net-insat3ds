In both downstream tasks, we adopt the same U-Net
architecture and training conﬁguration, and only vary the
input sequences during training/validation/testing among three
options: 1) the original FY-4A sequences with 15-min tempo-
ral resolution (raw); 2) 5-min sequences obtained by simple
temporal linear interpolation (linear); and 3) 5-min temporally
super-resolved sequences produced by METEOR-Net (our).
Since severe convective weather identiﬁcation and prediction
must extract convective signals that are closely related to the
occurrence of heavy surface precipitation from the temporal
evolution of satellite cloud ﬁelds, performance diﬀerences
under the same network architecture and training setup mainly
reﬂect diﬀerences in the completeness of physically relevant
information and the noise level carried by the respective input
sequences. Therefore, superior performance in these down-
stream tasks indicates that the input sequence provides a more
realistic and detailed description of convective development
and is more physically consistent with the independent surface
precipitation observations.

Fig. 13 presents the comparison results for the severe
convective weather prediction task. As expected, CSI and POD
decrease with increasing lead time for all methods, while
FAR increases. The METEOR-Net input (our) consistently
achieves the highest CSI at lead times of 1, 2, and 3 h, and
achieves the highest POD at 2 and 3 h, while maintaining the
lowest FAR at all three lead times. These ﬁndings demonstrate
that, under the independent constraint of surface precipita-
tion observations, the temporally super-resolved sequences
produced by METEOR-Net provide substantial beneﬁts for
short-term severe convection forecasting, rather than merely
yielding visually smoother images or ﬁtting noise.
