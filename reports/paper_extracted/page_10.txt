B. Experimental Setup
The METEOR-Net is conﬁgured with the following archi-
tectural parameters: The sequence branch of the MCE module
comprises three layers, while the skip branch consists of
one layer, both with 64 hidden units. The TFEE module
is structured with four layers and 256 hidden units. In the
EFFR module, 3 × 3 convolutional/deconvolutional kernels
are used for the reconstruction module, while 7 × 7 con-
volutional kernels are used for evolution-aware mapping and
pixel rearrangement in the fusion module. LeakyReLU [54]
is used as the primary activation function in most parts of
the network, except for weighting processes where Sigmoid
activation is applied for normalization, and the recurrent unit,
where Sigmoid and Tanh are used. In the region-weighted loss
function, α, β, and γ are set to 1, 2, and 5, respectively.
To ensure experimental fairness in comparative analysis, all
models used for comparison are implemented using oﬃcial
open-source code. Model training is conducted uniformly on
NVIDIA Tesla A100 GPUs with consistent hyperparameters:
batch size of 64, maximum training epochs of 200, Adam
optimizer with initial learning rate of 3 × 10−4. The learning
rate decreases when the validation set loss no longer decreases,
and training stops when it drops below 8 × 10−5.
C. Evaluation Metrics
The quantitative evaluation metrics for model performance
are categorized into two groups: image quality metrics and
meteorological evaluation metrics. Both groups are calculated
frame-wise and then averaged over all evaluated frames.
Image quality metrics include peak signal-to-noise ratio
(PSNR) [55], structural similarity index (SSIM) [56], and root
mean square error (RMSE). PSNR is based on mean square
error (mse) between ground-truth frames and reconstruction
frames, with higher values indicating smaller reconstruc-
tion deviations. SSIM quantiﬁes structural similarity between
ground truth and reconstructed frames, where higher values
denote greater similarity. RMSE quantiﬁes the pixel-level error
between the reconstructed frames and the ground truth frames,
where a lower value indicates smaller errors. These metrics
evaluate temporal super-resolution global visual ﬁdelity across
all channels (channel 7–14), failing to assess the critical recon-
struction performance of convective processes in geostationary
satellite temporal super-resolution.
Meteorological evaluation metrics include critical success
index (CSI), probability of detection (POD), and false alarm
rate (FAR), which are commonly used in precipitation now-
casting evaluation [57], [58], [59], [60]. CSI, POD, and FAR,
traditionally utilized in precipitation forecast evaluation, are
adapted for geostationary satellite cloud data assessment. Since
the 10.7-µm channel is situated within the infrared atmospheric
window and experiences minimal atmospheric absorption,
it serves as the primary channel for the identiﬁcation and
tracking of convective clouds. Quantitative evaluation results
from the 10.7-µm channel eﬀectively reﬂect the model’s per-
formance in reconstructing convective processes. Therefore,
the 10.7-µm channel is selected as the channel for assessing
the performance of convective reconstruction. Brightness tem-
perature thresholds of 273K, 241K, and 221K are established
to identify cold cloud, convective cloud, and strong convective
cloud regions [50], respectively. Given that lower cloud-top
brightness temperatures typically indicate stronger convec-
tive activity, pixels below these thresholds are designated
as positive samples. This enables the computation of hits
(reconstruction = 1 and truth = 1), misses (reconstruction = 0
and truth = 1), and false alarms (reconstruction = 1 and truth
= 0) for subsequent CSI, POD, and FAR calculations.
CSI, also known as the threat score, integrates hit, miss, and
false alarm events as
CSI = HitsHits + Misses + False Alarm. (25)
POD measures the model’s ability to capture actual events
as
POD = HitsHits + Misses. (26)
FAR quantiﬁes the proportion of incorrectly identiﬁed
events as
FAR = False AlarmHits + False Alarm. (27)
D. Multichannel Reconstruction Comparison Experiment
In this section, the performance of METEOR-Net in the
multichannel temporal super-resolution reconstruction task
will be evaluated and compared with traditional temporal
super-resolution methods and deep learning-based temporal
super-resolution methods. Traditional methods include linear
interpolation and optical ﬂow methods [61], where linear
interpolation serves as a baseline using bilinear interpolation
for intermediate frame generation, while the optical ﬂow
method uses the classical Farneback algorithm for dense
optical ﬂow ﬁeld estimation between adjacent frames. The
deep learning-based baselines include FLA VR, SSM-T, and
EMAIRA-VFI models. FLA VR, proposed by Kalluri et al.
[30], represents the SOTA performance in VFI algorithms.
SSM-T, recently developed by Vandal and Nemani [42] for
the GOES-R satellite, represents the current SOTA in tem-
poral super-resolution of geostationary satellite cloud data.
EMAIRA-VFI, proposed by Wang et al. [62] is a transformer-
based temporal super-resolution model for meteorological
data, representing advanced techniques in meteorological tem-
poral super-resolution.
